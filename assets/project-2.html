Sentiment Analysis of Political Social Media Posts using SVMs¶

Andrew Morgan¶

Sentiment analysis is a classical, popular classification task where the sentiment (e.g. positive, negative, neutral) of pieces of text is classified using machine learning. While state-of-the-art sentiment analysis experiments usually involve transformers (LLMs), I'd like to take a look at the performance of a traditional machine learning algorithm: the support vector machine. The task at hand is the classification of partisanship in social media posts from politicians (with two label classes: "partisan" and "neutral"). The goal is to train a model that is able to differentiate between text that is partisan and text that isn't; in other words, the model should develop an "understanding" of what makes certain language partisan. Some online spaces like Twitter/X seem to have grown more political in recent years, which is why this task interested me and why classifying partisanship specifically is relevant. Also, I found the challenge of trying to distill what makes text "partisan" a fascinating and layered challenge.

I will be using Figure Eight/Appen's Political Social Media Posts dataset from Kaggle, which was annotated by a number of contributors and appears reputable and well-maintained: https://www.kaggle.com/datasets/crowdflower/political-social-media-posts?resource=download. The data was provided by Appen (https://www.appen.com/), an established company (they claim 25+ years of industry experience) that provides annotated datasets for training AI models, among other things. Data quality is especially important in a domain as subjective as language.

Because the only obvious feature in this case is the text itself, I'll also be using a word vectorizer, or in other words, an algorithm that converts strings of text into feature vectors that encapsulate the meaning of the text. In line with my examination of traditional machine learning algorithms, I'll be looking at a traditional text vectorization method offered by scikit-learn: word count (also known as "bag-of-words") vectorization.

Now we have our labels encoded in a numpy array. Next, let's encode our text. While it only occupies one column, the text actually contains several features; that is, qualities of the text that help our model learn what makes the text partisan or neutral. What we need is a ___representation___ of the text as a vector of features. While there are endlessly complex ways of doing this (as language is endlessly complex), such as word2vec, let's use a simpler, traditional method, which is to represent the text as a collection of words and their respective counts in the text.

Before I encode the text, a quick word on stop words, which are words that provide little information about the text and are therefore excluded from the text representation. According to their own documentation, scikit-learn's list of stop words is unreliable; instead, I will use Natural Language Toolkit (NLTK)'s, another popular machine learning library designed particularly for natural language processing, list of stop words.

Now that we have our data preprocessed and prepared, let's get to training our model, the support vector machine (SVM).

Training a SVM / SVM Overview¶

I plan to use SVMs (support vector machines) because they adapt to high-dimensional scenarios well (because of kernels, discussed below). SVMs rely on finding the optimal hyperplane that separates the given examples (in our case, in a 1000-dimensional space, as we have 1000-dimensional feature vectors) such that the margin between the two label classes in the space is maximized. Out-of-the-box SVMs only function with linearly separable data, so a so-called "kernel function" is used to transform the features to a higher dimensional space where linear separability may exist and a hyperplane can be found. Of course, unless we are extremely lucky, our text feature space will not be linearly separable, so we'll use a kernel function to allow for the SVM to find an optimal non-linear hyperplane. Scikit-learn uses the radial basis function as the default kernel function, which transforms example data to infinite dimensions (but still finds the hyperplane in our 1000 dimensions) and makes the most sense for our high-dimensional task.

One potential negative in using SVMs is that they introduce some bias when training on imbalanced datasets (according to https://towardsdatascience.com/when-do-support-vector-machines-fail-3f23295ebef2/). I've attempted to counteract this by balancing the class weights when I initialize the SVM, which pre-empts the bias towards the minority class (partisan) by weighting partisan more than neutral.

Evaluation and Model Inspection¶

Now that we have the predictions, we can compute some metrics against our y_test data. Here, I'll use accuracy as well as precision, recall, and F1 score, which paint a fuller story of the model's performance than accuracy alone.

Recall is the rate at which the model correctly made a positive prediction against all actual positive labels in the test dataset, while precision is the rate at which the model correctly made a positive prediction against all actual correct and incorrect positive labels. The F1 score roughly combines the two into one metric. First, to visualize the model's performance, I'll generate a confusion matrix, which documents all of the true positive, false positive, true negative, and false negative predictions made by the model.

The performance appears fairly mediocre, though certainly much better than random guessing. The model appears to have had difficulty capturing the meaning of "partisan" as defined by the data. There is a slight skew towards over-predicting text as partisan (i.e., labeling partisan for neutral text), considering the lower precision for the partisan label. The precise reason for these struggles is difficult to interpret, given the high complexity of language, but I would wager that it has to do with the count vectorization process not preserving all of the semantic information of what made the text neutral or partisan (in other words, a loss of contextualization).

Let's manually look at some misclassified examples as well as some correct "partisan" classifications to try and get a better idea of why the model is missing this way. Note that I balanced the class weights when I initialized the model because of the class imbalance in the dataset, so there is a (intentional) bias toward predicting that the text is partisan.

My read on the model's performance is that, to an extent, it is disregarding the surrounding context of keywords (i.e., words with strong political meaning). One of the pitfalls of traditional text vectorization methods is that contextualization is not preserved well; this is why attention is such an important concept in modern text classification.

I'm noticing that the model is possibly interpreting "Obama" and adjacent words as being partisan - could we look at feature importances to check? Since our data is being transformed to a higher dimension, feature importance can't be retrieved directly, but we can estimate feature importance by determining permutation importance using scikit-learn. Permutation importance indirectly measures feature importance in the model by permuting each individual feature column and measuring the impact on the model - this allows us to estimate feature importance in our non-linear SVM.

Please note that my permutation_importance() setup takes about 20 minutes to run.

Note, too, that this permutation importance search is somewhat limited. The computational requirements of permutation_importance() on a significant number of features and examples is very high; I would rather have used the entire training set but limited it to only the first 100 examples.

I see that "obamacare" is one of the top twenty words, but I'm surprised not to see "obama" (this could be because of the limitations of permutation importance). Notice that some (political terms) make sense as important in determining partisan sentiment, like "msnbc" or "left", while others are more inscrutable, like "today" ("today" was the most common word overall in the dataset - this might indicate it should actually be excluded). Also, remember that there are 1000 word-count features making up our model and that the meaning of language can't ever be distilled to just a few of them, so each importance should be quite small.

Possible Next Steps?¶

Text classification is perhaps the most complex type of classification in more ways than one (very high-dimensional feature vectors, and there are many hyperparameters and dimensions in your experimental setup to optimize). Future experiments could be in the realm of optimizing the setup and hyperparameters. One approach I can think of now could be to only include in the feature list words that are in the top 100 most important features (as defined by permutation_importance()), which will theoretically reduce the noise in the model and potentially increase both the accuracy and training/testing time.

Impact¶

Classifying the partisanship of online social media posts (especially those of office-holding politicians) could be useful as a way to keep a tab on the growth of partisanship in our daily online dialogue (something that seems to have happened in the past decade). I would hypothesize that a dataset of political social media posts from 2012 would have many fewer posts classified as partisan than one from today, and a strong sentiment classifier model would be an efficient way to show that.

Classifying adjacent things, such as hate speech in political social media posts, could also be useful as a way of marking hateful content. A social media platform might want to use such an (accurate) classifier model as a way of efficiently marking or filtering out toxic content. A possible negative here, though, could be the use of such sentiment classification models for harmful censorship of content (though this issue is totally subjective).

Conclusion¶

While support vector machines are a powerful traditional method of machine learning, this project indicates that highly complex tasks like text classification may be beyond their scope and more suited for advanced ML architectures like transformers. Also, count vectorization fails to fully capture the contextual meaning of words when compared to modern methods like attention. In future work, I would like to compare the results of a modern machine learning model trained on this dataset using modern NLP/feature engineering methods to my current SVM setup.

Still, this project gave me an excellent look at not just SVMs but also common natural language processing methods and procedures (e.g., removing stop words). In particular, inspecting my trained model by examining classification probabilities gave good insight into its inner workings. Modeling something as complex and deeply saturated with meaning as language is a fascinating challenge to me, and attempting to with SVMs was a valuable experience despite its somewhat lackluster results.
